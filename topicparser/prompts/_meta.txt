I use a desktop tool that watches GitHub and X (Twitter) and gives me a ranked list
of things worth my attention in a subject I care about. It decides what to show me by
asking a language model to score every signal from 0 to 100 against a set of rules I
write. I want you to help me write those rules.

How the tool works, so you know the constraints:

- It collects raw signals: new GitHub repositories (name, description, star count)
  and tweets (author, text).

- Each signal is scored 0-100. Anything at 70 or above reaches my feed. The rest is
  discarded silently.

- The model doing the scoring is small and cheap. It anchors hard on whatever it
  reads FIRST and on whatever a section ENDS with, and it drifts toward giving
  everything the same middling score unless the rules push it apart.

- The output format, the language of the descriptions, and everything mechanical is
  already handled elsewhere. My rules describe TASTE only: what is interesting to me
  and what is noise.

Interview me until you understand my subject. Ask about:

- what I follow this subject for, in my own words;

- who else cares about it, and what they already know;

- categories I am sick of seeing, even though they match my subject;

- what makes something actually worth my attention: a launch, a novel idea, a number,
  someone being wrong, a thing I can try myself;

- subjects, languages or regions I never want to see;

- for tweets specifically: do I want raw facts I can work with myself, or am I happy
  with somebody's finished argument?

Ask one question at a time. Do not write the rules until you have enough to be
specific. Generic rules score everything the same and the feed becomes useless.

Then produce the rules in this shape, as plain text, no markdown:

  GATE A, the flood. Categories I never want, scored 40-55 however popular they are.
  Include a one-sentence test that separates the flood from the thing I do want,
  phrased in terms of the SUBJECT rather than a keyword: a tool that merely MENTIONS
  a category is not that category.

  GATE B, seen it before. Real and on-subject but one more of something that already
  exists: 50-60. Add the narrow exception that IS worth showing, and spell out WHY it
  is different. Naming a thing without a reason does not hold.

  GATE C, the 70+ bar. What actually earns my attention: genuine news, a novel idea,
  visible adoption, plus whatever is specific to my subject.

  HARD CAPS, always under 70 whatever else is true.

  For tweets: what makes a tweet worth building on.

Rules for you while writing it:

- Put the rejects FIRST and end the whole thing on the rule I least want ignored.

- Name concrete examples from my subject rather than abstractions.

- Never invent an interest I did not state.

- Length follows the subject. A narrow interest needs a short file; a broad one with
  many exceptions can run to several hundred lines. Do not pad it, and do not cut a
  rule that is doing real work just to make it shorter.
